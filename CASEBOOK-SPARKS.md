## Casebook: patterns and moves from other systems (sparks, not templates)

Short summaries of real cases from the SI Accountability Casebook (CASEBOOK.md), where
ordinary people were taken advantage of, and how it was found and fixed. Use them as ideas to consider:
- Phase 2: "Does this pattern show up here?" It only counts if THIS system's own documents or records show
  it. Never force a match, and don't mention a pattern that doesn't fit.
- Phase 3 and 4: "What worked elsewhere?" Label it "Idea from elsewhere" and name the case in one line.
- You didn't read these sources this session, so tag case facts [CHECK: SI casebook; source: ...].

### Patterns to look for (how harm gets hidden, and how it was found)
- **Hidden owners.** Shell companies whose names encode the real party (Kawhi Leonard's "KL2 Aspire LLC" in
  a bankruptcy creditor list; Meta's "Laidley LLC" for a Louisiana data center). Look at who really owns or
  benefits behind a company name.
- **Break the headline number into its parts.** DoorDash's "guaranteed pay": as the customer's tip went up,
  DoorDash's share went down and the worker's total stayed flat (NY AG $16.75M; NYC $131.5M).
- **Cross-check two public records.** A state senator's land deals vs. his votes on the bills that helped
  the project (Meta, Louisiana). Conflicts show up where two public records meet.
- **Follow who's owed money.** A bankruptcy creditor list revealed a hidden endorsement deal (Kawhi
  Leonard; NBA fined the Clippers $30M).
- **Who funds the watchdog.** The gold industry's "global authority" (LBMA) is funded and run by the
  refiners and banks it judges. Ask who pays any rater, certifier, or self-regulator.
- **Broad rights over your data.** A platform claims perpetual rights to user data; the cost shows up only
  when exposed (Meta/Cambridge Analytica: New Mexico verdict, $25B).
- **Public promise vs. fine print, or vs. what they say in court.** T-Mobile's "Price Guarantee" vs. a
  fine-print clause and a later price hike (NY AG settlement, refunds and credits). The LBMA calls itself
  the global standard-setter publicly, but "a small not-for-profit" in its court filings.
- **Backdated paperwork.** Transfers dated to just before a new transparency law took effect (Abramovich /
  Deloitte, Cyprus). Compare transaction dates with when the rule changed.
- **Lying to investigators.** EY told the SEC it had no cheating problem when it did; the cover-up became
  its own charge ($100M).

### How change got won
- **Build the referral, then hand it off.** Individual complaints -> a pattern in public information ->
  a regulator with subpoena power gets the internal records (DoorDash). An ordinary person doesn't have to
  prove the case, only package it for the right enforcer.
- **Go to the most aggressive enforcer with jurisdiction, not just the obvious one.** NYC's consumer and
  worker protection department won about 8x what the state AG did against DoorDash.
- **Ask for public disclosure where secrecy enabled the harm** (e.g. public registers of who owns
  companies, after the Abramovich case; or "publish how often you use this discretion").
- **Insiders have a paid channel.** The SEC whistleblower program (EY).

### What people did for themselves
- **Claim the remedy.** Settlements often need a claim (DoorDash NY AG version) or pay out automatically
  (NYC version); T-Mobile customers could choose refunds or credits.
- **Don't leave idle money with a custodian that isn't insured;** move it somewhere protected.
- **Get the promise in writing before you commit,** and compare it with the fine print and your bill.
